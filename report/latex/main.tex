\begin{abstract}
We study how optimization constraints and reward sources shape what Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct learns under LoRA post-training, holding prompts, decoding, seeds and budgets fixed across every comparison. After correcting three objective defects in the released code (DPO reference-margin sign, PPO $\max$ instead of $\min$, batch-wide instead of per-group GRPO normalization), we find a recurring gap between optimized metrics and intended behaviour. DPO raises held-out preference accuracy to 0.64 with negligible KL ($\sim\!10^{-4}$), but learns a \emph{shorter-is-preferred} bias that persists, and strengthens, on length-balanced data, pointing to the summed-log-probability objective rather than the dataset. Short PPO and GRPO continuations move the policy so little that clipping never activates and no held-out difference exceeds its 95\% confidence interval, while the learned reward assigns high scores to a factually inverted answer and to truncated, wrong-language output. Dr.-GRPO reallocates gradient mass toward long completions (long/short gradient-norm ratio 4.33 vs.\ 0.18). On XSTest, the 3B AI judge never emits \textsc{over\_refusal}: a blind manual audit of 240 responses finds 33--37\% over-refusal on benign prompts for every policy where the judge reports 0\% (agreement 0.68, $\kappa=0.45$). On GSM8K, an exact verifier is perfectly outcome-sensitive but format-bound, whereas the pairwise AI judge detects only 25\% of wrong final answers, never detects corrupted reasoning, and prefers persuasive filler 25\% of the time.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Post-training turns a next-token predictor into a policy shaped by preferences, learned rewards or verifiable outcomes. Whether an objective improves its own score is rarely in doubt; the question is what behaviour that score buys, what drifts from the reference model, and which failures the training signal cannot see. We compare three optimizers---offline Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)~\cite{rafailov2023dpo}, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)~\cite{schulman2017ppo,ouyang2022instructgpt} and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)~\cite{shao2024deepseekmath,liu2025understanding}---then audit safety calibration on XSTest~\cite{rottger2023xstest} and contrast verifiable (RLVR) with AI-feedback (RLAIF) rewards~\cite{deepseek2025r1,lee2024rlaif}. Our unifying question: \emph{how do optimization constraints and reward sources shape what the model learns to optimize, and when do measured objectives fail to match the behaviour we want?} Every claim below traces to a file in \texttt{results/} produced by a single command in the repository README.

\section{Experimental setup}
\textbf{Models and data.} All policies are Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct~\cite{qwen2.5} with rank-8 LoRA~\cite{hu2021lora} on $q,v$ projections; the frozen reference is the base model with the adapter disabled. Rewards come from a fixed 1.5B UltraFeedback~\cite{cui2023ultrafeedback} reward model (RM); judges are Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (4-bit). Data, prompt pools, checkpoints and caches are the course release. Runs used one Tesla T4 (fp16) on Kaggle.

\textbf{Objective validation.} Unit tests derived from the manual's equations fail on the released code and pass after three fixes: (i) DPO logit used $\beta(\Delta_\pi+\Delta_{\text{ref}})$ instead of $\beta(\Delta_\pi-\Delta_{\text{ref}})$, which makes the initial loss depend on the reference margin instead of equalling $\log 2$; (ii) PPO took $\max(\rho A,\text{clip}(\rho)A)$, removing the trust region; (iii) GRPO standardized rewards over the whole batch, ignoring prompt groups. A further test checks that our micro-batched GRPO step reproduces the released full-batch loss and gradients exactly.

\textbf{Common protocol.} For every Task 1--3 condition, held-out behaviour is one sampled response per fixed prompt (seed 6304, $T{=}0.7$, top-$p{=}0.9$; first 100 held-out DPO prompts with a 256-token cap, or first 64 held-out RL prompts with a 768-token cap). Metrics follow the manual: KL is the token-weighted sampled estimator $\mathbb{E}[\log\pi_\theta-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}]$ (it can be slightly negative when drift is tiny), entropy is the exact full-vocabulary token entropy, and length counts response tokens. Because one sample per prompt is noisy (RM score s.d.\ $\approx$1.5), we report \emph{paired} differences on identical prompts with 95\% CIs. Following common RLHF implementation practice~\cite{huang2024nplus}, in PPO/GRPO LoRA dropout is disabled so the importance ratio is exactly 1 before the first step, and all forks share one seeded prompt schedule (generated-token budgets agree within 2.4\%).

\textbf{Deviations, stated once.} 3.7\% of DPO pairs (56/1500 standard, 62/1500 balanced, 10/300 eval) are dropped by a deterministic filter because their prompts exceed the 768-token budget (the released encoder raises on them). Task 4 judging batches the released prompt/parser with greedy decoding. The manual audit labels each distinct response once and propagates the label to every (prompt, policy) pair that produced it (110 labels $\rightarrow$ 240 pairs).

\textbf{Preference strength and drift (RQ1).} $\beta$ has no detectable effect on what is learned at this budget (Tables~\ref{tab:dpo}, \ref{tab:beta}): accuracy, margin and KL are flat, the per-pair margins of the $\beta{=}0.03$ and $\beta{=}0.3$ models correlate at 0.98, and the apparent rise in RM score is not significant ($\beta{=}0.3$ minus $\beta{=}0.03$: $+0.07\pm0.09$). The reason is the operating regime. The DPO gradient is $-\beta\,\sigma(-\beta m)\nabla m$, and $\beta$ regularises only through $\sigma(-\beta m)$, which down-weights pairs that are already separated and makes the optimum the KL-anchored $\pi^\ast\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}\exp(r/\beta)$. Here $\beta m\le0.05$, so $\sigma(-\beta m)\approx\tfrac12$ for every pair and $\beta$ merely scales the gradient --- a scale that gradient clipping (active on every step for $\beta\ge0.1$) and Adam's per-parameter normalisation both remove. Parameter updates, and hence margins, KL and samples, are therefore nearly identical across $\beta$; the relationship is flat rather than monotonic over the tested range, and would only appear near convergence. More data, not larger $\beta$, is what moves the model: the one-epoch run grows the margin 2.6$\times$ without raising accuracy, and it does so by lowering \emph{both} responses (chosen $-0.45$, rejected $-0.85$ nats), the likelihood-displacement behaviour of DPO~\cite{razin2024unintentional} that drives the length effect below.

\textbf{Length confounding (RQ2).} Table~\ref{tab:length} separates the dataset from the policy. The \emph{dataset} carries a real but weak length correlation: a content-blind ``longer wins'' rule scores 0.570 on the standard training pairs, and the balanced set removes it. The \emph{policy} learns the opposite direction: both models rank pairs best when the rejected answer is longer and below chance when the preferred one is (a 0.22 gap, $\approx$2.8 s.e.), and the same holds on the regular held-out set for all five DPO models. A data explanation predicts a preference for \emph{longer} answers that disappears under balancing; instead the bias \emph{strengthens} when length is uninformative, which points to the objective. The margin sums per-token log-ratio changes, and because training lowers likelihood on average, every response carries a term $\approx-c\,|y|$, so the margin contains $-c(|y^+|-|y^-|)$. This predicts a linear dependence on the length gap, and the fitted slopes reproduce the strata (predicted $0.00/0.24/0.47$ vs.\ observed $-0.07/0.17/0.61$ for standard); the balanced slope is $3.2\times$ steeper, matching its $2.2\times$ larger per-token displacement: with no length signal in its data, nothing counteracts the displacement. Truncation is ruled out (no scored response reached the 768-token budget), and per-token normalisation is not a fix --- it \emph{reverses} the bias (0.705 vs.\ 0.481), because displacement is not uniform across tokens. Prior work reports DPO exploiting length toward verbosity~\cite{park2024disentangling,singhal2023long}; with a weak dataset correlation and early stopping, the objective's own length term dominates here instead. Balancing therefore changes \emph{which} pairs are ranked correctly, but not how long the model answers: generated length is unchanged ($|\Delta|\le3$ tokens), with the caveat that 45--50\% of generations reach the 256-token cap, so only shortening could have been detected.

\textbf{Reward vs.\ quality (RQ3).} Table~\ref{tab:t1qual} collects cases where the aligned policy has a stronger signal but a worse or uninformative outcome. Word-limit compliance drops only after a full epoch (5/10$\rightarrow$4/10): the preference pairs reward completeness, and none rewards brevity under an explicit limit, so more fitting pushes toward elaboration. The RM is not systematically fooled by length ($\rho(\text{RM},\text{tokens})\approx0$), but individual scores are unreliable, and part of the accuracy metric measures tied labels. Offline preference optimization fits exactly the label distribution it is given --- its noise, its omissions, and the objective's own length term --- and, because it never samples from the policy, nothing in training detects behaviours the pairs do not encode.

\textbf{Standard continuation.} Figure~\ref{fig:ppo} and Table~\ref{tab:std} show a continuation that barely moves the policy. Per-update reward tracks the sampled prompt rather than a trend; KL to the reference stays within $\pm10^{-3}$; entropy fluctuates with the prompt (0.3--2.3); and the clip fraction is exactly zero because the importance ratio never leaves $[0.958,1.043]$. With $\eta=3\cdot10^{-6}$ and a single rollout per update, each step is too small to reach the trust region. 40\% of rollouts hit the 512-token cap and receive the missing-EOS penalty, which produces the bimodal lengths. The released critic remains poorly calibrated (median explained variance $-4.7$), so GAE~\cite{schulman2016gae} advantages are dominated by the terminal reward rather than by accurate value estimates; reward improving would not imply the critic is accurate. Held-out behaviour is indistinguishable from the midpoint.

\textbf{Clipping (RQ1).} The two halves of Table~\ref{tab:ppo} answer different questions. On the fixed cached batch, $\epsilon$ sets how much of a batch is constrained: 15.4\% of tokens fall outside the band at $\epsilon=0.05$ against 2.1\% at 0.5, and about half of those have their gradient zeroed (the min selects the clipped branch only when the advantage sign pushes the ratio further out). This is pure geometry: the cached old policy sits at a fixed approx-KL of 0.107 from the midpoint, and four inner steps at this learning rate do not change the fractions. In the matched forks, however, the policy never moves far enough for that geometry to act: the largest ratio deviation is 2.8\% and the largest update KL $7.8\cdot10^{-6}$, so no token is clipped even at $\epsilon=0.05$ and the three forks are \emph{bit-identical}. Clipping can only stabilise updates that would otherwise exceed the band; at this step size there are none, so $\epsilon$ has no effect on stability.

\textbf{KL pressure (RQ2) and over-optimization (RQ3).} Weakening KL pressure produces no measurable drift (Table~\ref{tab:ppo}): every held-out difference lies inside its CI and the logged KL stays at the $10^{-4}$ noise floor. This is consistent with the clipping result --- the KL penalty $\beta_{\text{KL}}(\log\pi_\theta-\log\pi_{\text{ref}})$ is a per-token cost of size $\beta_{\text{KL}}\cdot10^{-4}$, negligible next to terminal rewards of order 1, so removing it changes almost nothing over 8 updates. The first observable divergence is in individual responses and lengths ($\beta_{\text{KL}}=0$ and 0.2 average 293 tokens vs.\ 311), not in aggregate reward, KL or entropy. We find no evidence of reward over-optimization, because the policy barely leaves the reference and over-optimization appears only as KL grows~\cite{gao2023scaling}. The learned reward is nonetheless unreliable \emph{at} the reference (Table~\ref{tab:t2qual}): it scores a legally reversed answer far above a cautious one, so a reward increase in a longer run could not be read as a quality increase without held-out checks such as Task~4.

\textbf{Group size (RQ1).} Comparing at equal generations (Table~\ref{tab:k}), the informative-group rate is \emph{not} the binding constraint under a continuous reward: almost every group has non-zero variance at any $K$. What $K$ changes is the \emph{quality} of the relative signal. A group's mean is a noisy baseline estimate, and its sampling error shrinks as $1/\sqrt{K}$: weak groups fall from 26\% to 4\%, the within-group s.d.\ approaches the prompt's true spread (0.39$\rightarrow$0.62), and the correlation of a group's advantages with the full 8-sample advantages rises from 0.62 to 0.88. Medium and easy prompts benefit most (weak groups vanish at $K{=}8$); hard prompts keep 12.5\% weak groups because one hard prompt's completions all receive nearly the same reward. The binarized column shows the regime the manual warns about: with a pass/fail reward, a group is informative only if it contains both outcomes, with probability $1-p^K-(1-p)^K$, which is zero for prompts where every completion fails (all hard prompts) and grows steeply with $K$ for medium prompts (0.41$\rightarrow$1.00) --- the low-resolution setting, typical of verifiable rewards~\cite{deepseek2025r1}, where larger $K$ is worth its generation cost.

\textbf{Sequence normalization (RQ2).} The two losses differ only in the per-completion weight on the summed token objective: $1/T_k$ gives every completion the same total weight regardless of length, so each token of a long completion counts less; $1/L_{\max}$ gives every token the same weight, so long completions receive proportionally more gradient. Table~\ref{tab:norm} measures exactly this on identical completions: canonical GRPO puts 72\% of gradient norm on the shortest tercile, Dr.-GRPO puts 55\% on the longest, a 24-fold change in the long/short ratio. Over 8 matched updates this reallocation did not change behaviour --- held-out reward, length and KL are all within noise --- for two reasons visible in the data: the updates are too small to move the policy (as in Task 2), and masking removes the longest completions from both losses (11/32 and 12/32 were truncated), so the completions Dr.-GRPO would up-weight most never contribute. Normalization changes \emph{where} gradient goes; whether that becomes a length or quality change depends on training long enough and on the cap not masking the long tail.

\textbf{Manual audit (RQ3).} Table~\ref{tab:conf} shows that judge errors are systematic rather than random. Agreement is high on unsafe prompts (0.85), where refusing is correct and the judge's two-label behaviour happens to fit, and low on safe prompts (0.51). \section{Cross-task discussion}
\textbf{Preference strength versus drift.} Across DPO $\beta$, PPO $\beta_{\text{KL}}$ and GRPO normalization, measured KL to the reference stays at $10^{-4}$--$10^{-3}$; the regularizers were never the binding constraint at these budgets. What changed was \emph{where} the objective puts its weight: DPO's $\beta$ only rescales a gradient that clipping and Adam renormalise, so far from convergence it changes neither drift nor what is learned, and GRPO normalization moves gradient mass between short and long completions without moving the policy measurably.

\textbf{Offline versus online feedback.} DPO's fixed pairs let it fit labels efficiently (0.64 accuracy after 91 steps), including label noise (39 tied pairs) and an implicit length bias. PPO and GRPO learn from their own samples, so their signal depends on the RM's judgement of current outputs --- and that RM rewards a factually inverted legal answer and truncated off-language text. Online methods inherit the evaluator's blind spots directly.

\textbf{Optimization bias.} Three distinct length effects appear: DPO's summed-log-ratio margin favours shorter responses even on length-balanced data; PPO's 512-token cap truncates 40\% of rollouts and triggers the missing-EOS penalty; GRPO's masking and $1/T_k$ normalization decide whether long completions contribute gradient at all. None of these is visible in the scalar reward.

\textbf{Safety calibration and reward-source design.} Better preference or reward metrics did not correspond to better calibration, and the automated safety metric hid a 33--37\% over-refusal rate that a 240-response manual audit exposed. Exact verification gives perfect outcome sensitivity at negligible cost but is blind to reasoning and brittle to format; AI feedback covers more responses but adds order noise, style bias and five orders of magnitude more compute. In every task, the number that was optimized or reported was a partial view of the behaviour we wanted.

\textbf{Limitations.} Continuations are 8--20 single-prompt updates, so RL ablations have little statistical power; held-out sets are 64--100 prompts with one sample each; the RM and judges are small; one seed per condition.

\section{Conclusion}
Correcting three objective defects was necessary for any of these comparisons to be meaningful, but correct objectives still optimize partial proxies. At this scale the clearest effects are not differences between optimizers but properties of the signals: DPO's length-sensitive implicit reward, an RM that rewards confident errors, a safety judge that cannot express over-refusal, and a verifier that grades format as strictly as arithmetic. Reliable post-training evaluation needs paired, uncertainty-aware comparisons and at least one independent check of every automated judge.
